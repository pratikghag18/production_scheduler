Node tree — four ways to remove the ambiguity

Same data in every panel: Plant 1 on the four-level Standard Plant shape, Plant 2 on the two-level Compact Plant shape. That mix is the case worth designing for — it is the one the current tree cannot express.

What is actually ambiguous today

Packing Line
Assembly and Packing Line sit at the same indent and are not the same kind of thing. Assembly is a Department; Packing Line is a Line, and a schedulable one — work gets booked on it. Before per-site shapes, depth 2 always meant Department. It no longer does, and indentation is the only thing the tree encodes.

FixesLevel is the column header, so it cannot be misread. Stays scannable at hundreds of cells, where one long indented list stops working. CostsYou lose the whole-tree overview — no seeing Cell 1 and Cell 7 at once. Wants horizontal room. Drag-to-reparent (P1-5g) gets harder, not easier. EffortLargest. FixesNothing to infer at all, and it sorts and filters. Familiar from a spreadsheet, and it is the natural preview format for CSV import when that lands. CostsTwo shapes do not share a column set — see the seam above, where Plant 2 has no Department and its schedulable level is Line, not Work Cell. Either a separator per shape or one table per shape; neither is tidy. Editing structure here is worse than in a tree. EffortMedium, and it is a second view rather than a replacement.

And the two decisions you already made

Renamed to “Site Structure”, picker merged into one card.

Site Structure

One card instead of two peers, so the coupling is structural rather than something to infer: the level list visibly belongs to the structure named above it.

“Site Structure” also reads correctly in the flow that matters — the shape dropdown when you add a root node becomes “which site structure does Plant 2 use?”, which is a question a plant manager can answer.

Note the level rows now carry the schedulable marker as a chip rather than a radio buried at the end of the row — same idiom as the level labels in the tree, so the two cards speak the same visual language.
